# Modern Marathon Training Science

This document defines the training science principles that govern Athlos plan generation. It is grounded in peer-reviewed research (2022–2024 meta-analyses where available) and modern elite coaching methodology. Where Pfitzinger's *Advanced Marathoning* conflicts with this document, this document wins.

---

## Core Physiological Principles

### 1. What Determines Marathon Performance

Marathon performance is primarily determined by three factors in descending order of race-day relevance:

1. **Lactate threshold (LT)** — the pace you can sustain without accumulating lactate. Correlates with marathon time at r=0.91 in recreational runners.
2. **Running economy (RE)** — how efficiently you convert oxygen to forward movement. At the sub-elite level, RE may matter as much as LT.
3. **VO2max** — sets the ceiling. A higher ceiling means more room for LT and RE development, but VO2max alone doesn't win marathons.

**Implication:** Training emphasis should follow this order. VO2max work raises the ceiling early; the final weeks belong to LT and marathon-specific work.

## Intensity Distribution

### The Model: Pyramidal Base, Polarized Peak

Elite marathon runners predominantly train with a **pyramidal distribution** — Zone 1 (easy) > Zone 2 (moderate) > Zone 3 (hard) — particularly during base phases. As the race approaches (final 8 weeks), training shifts toward **polarized** — minimizing Zone 2 gray-zone work in favor of a sharper easy/hard contrast.

The strict 80/20 polarized framing is an oversimplification. Observational data on 119,452 marathon runners shows pyramidal is the dominant pattern among fast runners, not strict 80/20. However, the underlying principle — **avoid the gray zone** — is well-supported.

**By phase:**
- **Base:** Zone 1 dominant. Zone 2 moderate work is acceptable (medium-long runs). Avoid making easy days moderately hard.
- **Build:** Zone 1 dominant. Quality sessions clearly Zone 3. Reduce Zone 2.
- **Peak:** Polarized. Easy days genuinely easy. Hard days at threshold or MP. Gray zone minimized.
- **Taper:** Zone 1 dominant, 1 light quality session to maintain feel.

**Sources:**
- Filipas et al. (SJMSS 2022): [PMC9299127](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9299127/)
- Polarized vs. threshold meta-analysis (2024): [PMC11329428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11329428/)
- Marathon runner intensity distribution (PubMed 2024): [39616560](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39616560/)

## Periodization: Phase Structure and Quality Session Sequencing

### The Correct Arc

The evidence-backed periodization arc for marathon training — drawn from Daniels' *Running Formula*, Canova's marathon block methodology, and observational data on sub-elite runners — is:

| Phase | Duration | Quality Focus | Sessions/Week |
|-------|----------|---------------|---------------|
| General Fitness | Optional, 20+ week plans | None — aerobic base only | 0 |
| Base | ~30–40% of plan | Threshold/tempo (introduce LT work) | 1 |
| Build | ~25–30% of plan | Threshold + VO2max intervals (VO2max as sharpener); MP enters late Build | 1–2 |
| Peak | ~15–20% of plan | MP dominant + threshold support; VO2max reduced/eliminated | 2 |
| Taper | 3 weeks (full/half) | 1 light tempo in week 1; 0 after | 0–1 |

### Why This Order

**Threshold before VO2max, not the reverse.** The Pfitz model (and an earlier version of this doc) prescribed VO2max intervals in Base and early Build, then shifted to MP in Peak. The evidence leans the other way:

- Threshold work builds the aerobic base and sustainable pace foundation first.
- VO2max intervals are most effective as a **sharpening stimulus** in mid-Build after threshold capacity is established.
- The final 6–8 weeks should be marathon-specific: MP work should dominate, VO2max work reduced or eliminated.

Running VO2max intervals in the Peak phase — the final 8 weeks — is a Pfitz-specific choice that is not optimal for marathon preparation. VO2max intervals are most relevant to 5K–10K racing.

**MP work must begin early enough.** A 2024 analysis of 92 sub-elite marathon training plans (PMC11065819) found runners average 50–75 total miles at marathon pace over the final 12 weeks. Pfitz's ~14 total miles of MP work across 12 weeks is dramatically below evidence-based plans. MP work should enter in late Build and dominate Peak.

### Quality Session Types by Phase

| Phase | Session 1 | Session 2 | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-----------|-------|
| General Fitness | — | — | Easy volume only |
| Base | Tempo (threshold) | — | 1x/week; alternate with easy long |
| Build (early) | Tempo | Intervals (VO2max) | 2x/week as fitness builds |
| Build (late) | Tempo | MP | MP replaces intervals |
| Peak | MP | Tempo | MP is primary; tempo supports LT |
| Taper W1 | Light tempo | — | Short, easy; maintain feel |
| Taper W2+ | — | — | Easy only |

### Recovery Weeks

Recovery weeks every 4th week are well-supported. Volume: 30–40% reduction from prior peak (not from the prior week).

**Quality sessions on recovery weeks:** For advanced runners (this platform's target), **1 short, easy quality session** is acceptable and common in elite programs. Dropping all quality for recovery weeks is appropriate for newer or fatigued runners, but should not be an absolute rule for experienced athletes. Athlos targets advanced runners; the policy should be 0–1 quality sessions on recovery weeks based on phase (0 in taper recovery, 1 short in Base/Build recovery).

## Long Runs

### Structure

Two categories of long runs in a well-designed plan:

1. **~75% of long runs: genuinely easy.** Zone 1, 55–75% of 5K pace. Primary purpose: mitochondrial development, fat oxidation, tendon/connective tissue adaptation.
2. **~25% of long runs (1 every 3–4 weeks in Build/Peak): include MP segments.** Format: fast-finish (last 25–30% at MP) or interspersed MP intervals. Builds specific marathon fatigue tolerance.

MP-in-long-runs recommendations are coaching consensus supported by physiology. There is no RCT directly comparing all-easy vs. MP-segmented long runs on marathon outcomes.

### Distance

Peak long run: 20–22 miles (32–35 km) for full marathon preparation. **Total training load, not peak long run distance, best predicts late-race performance.** Cumulative volume in the 12-week build matters more than whether the peak run is 20 or 22 miles.

## Strength Training

### Evidence

Among the best-supported performance claims in modern running research:

- **PMC11052887 (2024, 31 studies, 652 runners):** Combined heavy load (≥80% 1RM) + plyometrics produced the largest running economy gains (ES = −0.426, moderate effect). Heavy load alone: significant effect (ES = −0.266). Plyometrics alone: no significant effect at marathon-relevant speeds.
- **PMC9653533 (2022):** Heavy resistance outperforms plyometrics for running economy (g = −0.32 vs −0.13) and time-trial performance (g = −0.24 vs −0.17). Optimal protocol: ≥80–90% 1RM, 10–14 week interventions.

### Key Finding

**Heavy resistance training is the primary mechanism. Plyometrics are a useful complement** (particularly at slower marathon paces), not co-equal. The platform should emphasize heavy resistance as the performance intervention.

### Scheduling

- 2×/week resistance training on easy run days (after the run; not adjacent to long run or quality sessions)
- Reduce to 1×/week in Peak (session furthest from the long run)
- 1×/week in Taper Week 1 only; 0 from Taper Week 2 onward
- 0 in Race Week
- Never on quality session days or the day immediately before or after a quality session

> For full phase-by-phase programming details, see `docs/training-science/strength-training-science.md`.

**Sources:**
- [PMC11052887](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11052887/) — Strength training for runners meta-analysis (2024)
- [PMC9653533](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9653533/) — Heavy resistance vs. plyometrics (2022)

## Volume Progression

### Ramp Rate

The 10% weekly increase rule is a useful rough heuristic but **is not scientifically validated as a specific threshold:**
- A 2007 RCT found identical ~20% injury rates in runners following the 10% rule vs. more aggressive ramp.
- A 2012 study found uninjured runners increased volume by ~22%/week on average.
- What IS supported: avoiding single-session spikes above 110% of the prior month's longest run (increases injury risk 64%).

**Athlos approach:** Use linear interpolation from starting volume to peak volume over the pre-taper weeks, with every-4th-week recovery dips. This guarantees the plan reaches peak volume by the start of taper. The ramp is not strictly 10% per week; it's determined by the gap between starting and target volume over the available weeks.

### Recovery Week Depth

30–40% volume reduction from prior peak every 4th week. The Athlos implementation uses 70% of the target trajectory for that week (effectively a 30% dip), which is at the conservative end but within evidence range.

## What Pfitz Gets Right (Retained)

- Mileage is the primary performance lever
- Long runs are critical; 20+ miles multiple times is sound for full marathon
- Phase structure naming and duration (Base → Build → Peak → Taper)
- Recovery weeks every 4th week
- Strength on easy days, never quality days

## What Pfitz Gets Wrong (Overridden)

1. **Periodization order:** Pfitz puts LT work early and VO2max late (in Peak). Correct order is threshold first, VO2max as mid-Build sharpener, MP dominant in Peak.
2. **MP volume:** ~14 miles of MP work in 12 weeks is dramatically below sub-elite norms (~50–75 miles). MP should dominate the final 6–8 weeks.
3. **Intensity distribution:** "General aerobic" runs at 75–84% max HR push into the gray zone. This generates fatigue without commensurate adaptation.
4. **Strength as supplementary:** Pfitz presents strength as optional. It is a first-class performance intervention.

## Gaps and Uncertainties

- Phase sequencing order has limited RCT evidence specifically for marathon runners. Most evidence is observational or inferred from shorter-cycle endurance studies.
- Exact "correct" total MP mileage over 18–20 weeks is not established by controlled trials.
- Polarized training studies primarily use cyclists or shorter-distance runners; marathon-specific RCTs are limited.
- Whether 0 or 1 quality session on recovery weeks is optimal is practically unstudied in RCTs.
