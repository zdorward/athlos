# Modern Marathon Science

This document outlines the training science principles that inform Athlos plan generation. It summarizes what Pfitzinger's *Advanced Marathoning* gets right, where it falls short, and what modern research prescribes instead.

---

## What Pfitz Gets Right

These Pfitzinger principles are well-supported by evidence and are retained as-is:

- **Mileage is the primary performance lever.** More aerobic volume → better marathon performance. Pfitz's emphasis on building to 55–100+ miles/week is sound.
- **Long runs are critical.** Running 20+ miles multiple times in a training cycle is well-supported for full marathon preparation. Long run targets relative to weekly mileage (30–35% of peak week) are appropriate.
- **Phase structure.** The Base → Build → Peak → Taper arc matches how elite programs are organized. Phase durations are reasonable.
- **10% weekly volume increase rule.** A conservative ramp rate that reduces injury risk.
- **Recovery weeks every 4th week.** Correct in principle; weekly monotony kills athletes.

## Where Pfitz Falls Short

### 1. Specificity Deficit (Critical)

**The problem:** In Pfitzinger's 12-week 18/70 plan, a runner completes approximately **14 total miles of marathon-pace running** — one 12-mile MP segment embedded in a 20-miler plus a brief dress rehearsal. Everything else is threshold or VO2max work.

**The research:** Marathon performance is dominated by lactate threshold, not VO2max. Lactate threshold correlates with marathon finishing time at r=0.91 vs. r=0.63 for VO2max in recreational runners. The marathon is run at 65–85% VO2max — it's a threshold event, not a VO2max event.

**What modern coaching prescribes:** Renato Canova's approach for a 2:09 marathoner included ~109 miles of marathon-specific work in a 9-week block. The ratio of marathon-pace to race-pace work is an order of magnitude higher than Pfitz. Even for age-group runners, a plan with 10–20% of total volume at or near MP in the Peak phase is more specific than what Pfitz prescribes.

**Implication for Athlos:** MP workouts should be the dominant quality stimulus in the Peak phase. Build phase should layer in threshold work. VO2max intervals should appear earlier (Base/Build) to raise the ceiling, not late (Peak) when specificity should dominate.

- Nate Jenkins (sub-2:17 marathoner, Canova athlete): [Canova vs Pfitzinger](http://nateruns.blogspot.com/2018/07/canova-marathon-training-vs-pfitzingers.html)
- Runners Connect: [Lactate Threshold vs VO2max](https://runnersconnect.net/lactate-vo2max/)

### 2. Periodization Inversion

**The problem:** Pfitz prescribes lactate threshold work in the Base and Build phases, then shifts to VO2max intervals (800m–1200m repeats at 5K pace) in the Peak phase — the final 8 weeks before the race. VO2max intervals are most appropriate for 5K–10K racing.

**What modern coaching prescribes:** The correct periodization arc for a marathon is:
- **Early (Base):** Aerobic development, introduce VO2max work to raise the ceiling
- **Mid (Build):** Shift toward threshold and MP work
- **Late (Peak):** Marathon-specific work dominates — MP long runs, threshold runs, MP segments. VO2max work is reduced or eliminated.

This is roughly what Daniels' *Running Formula* prescribes and what Canova's "special endurance" blocks achieve. It follows the specificity principle: train more specifically to your goal race as the race approaches.

**Implication for Athlos:** Quality session type distribution should shift over the plan:
- Base: tempo (threshold)
- Build: mix of tempo + intervals (VO2max)
- Peak: intervals (VO2max) reduced, MP dominant
- Taper: easy + one light tempo

- [LetsRun: Why 5K training in Pfitz last phase?](https://www.letsrun.com/forum/flat_read.php?thread=9708210)

### 3. Intensity Distribution: The Gray Zone Problem

**The problem:** Pfitz's plans are lactate-threshold heavy. The "general aerobic" runs at 75–84% max HR and medium-long runs at "endurance pace" sit in what exercise scientists call the **gray zone** — too fast to be truly easy (Zone 1), too slow to produce the highest-end adaptations (Zone 3). These runs generate meaningful fatigue without commensurate adaptation.

Fellrnr.com's training monotony analysis found Pfitz's 70–85 mpw plans carry "high" overtraining risk scores, and 85+ mpw plans "high to very high," in part because of this intensity distribution.

**What modern research prescribes:** Elite endurance athletes across sports gravitate toward a **polarized** distribution — roughly 80% Zone 1 (easy, conversational) and 20% Zone 3 (hard quality sessions), with minimal gray-zone work. Key studies:
- A 2007 study found polarized training improved performance by 4.2% vs. 2.9% for a threshold-emphasis group.
- A 2014 Muñoz et al. study found polarized training improved 10K times by 5.0% vs. 3.6%.
- A Frontiers in Physiology meta-analysis (PMC4621419) confirmed polarized training produces superior VO2max and performance gains with less accumulated fatigue.

**Implication for Athlos:** Easy runs should be genuinely easy — Zone 1, conversational. Medium-long runs at "endurance pace" (the gray zone) should be used sparingly. Quality sessions should be clearly hard: threshold, MP, or VO2max — not moderately hard.

**Sources:**
- [Frontiers in Physiology: Polarized vs Threshold Training (PMC4621419)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4621419/)

### 4. Strength Training: Not Integrated, Underemphasized

**The problem:** Pfitz prescribes 2x/week resistance and 3x/week core in Chapter 4, but the training plans themselves don't show strength as scheduled workouts. It's presented as supplementary, not integrated into load management. The practical result: most runners skip it.

**What modern research prescribes:** Recent meta-analyses have elevated strength training from "injury prevention" to "performance enhancement":
- A 2022 Sports Medicine meta-analysis found both heavy resistance and plyometric training **significantly improve running economy** (the efficiency measure most predictive of marathon performance).
- A 2024 RCT (PMID 40016936) found maximal strength + plyometric training improved **running economy durability** — the ability to maintain economy late in a long run — which is directly relevant to marathon performance.
- A 2023 Sports Medicine meta-analysis found high-load strength training is particularly effective for improving running economy at **high speeds** (sub-3:00 to sub-2:30 marathon pace).

**Pfitzinger's own scheduling rule (implied):** Strength is prescribed "after an aerobic warm-up" — meaning it pairs with running, not on rest days. His hard/easy framework implies strength should never fall on quality session days or adjacent to the long run.

**Correct integration:**
- 2x/week resistance training on easy run days (after the run)
- 3x/week core work on easy/rest days
- Reduce to 1x/week resistance in taper
- 0 resistance in race week
- Scheduler assigns strength automatically — not user-configured

**Sources:**
- [PMC: Resistance vs Plyometric for Running Economy (PMC9653533)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9653533/)
- [PMC: Strength Training for Distance Runners Meta-Analysis (PMC11052887)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11052887/)
- [PubMed: Running Economy Durability RCT (PMID 40016936)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40016936/)

### 5. Recovery: Too Sparse at High Mileage

**The problem:** At 70–85+ mpw, Pfitz plans have very few true rest days. The "recovery" weeks are not easy enough relative to peak weeks. Foster et al. (1998, Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise) identified training monotony scores above 2.0 as associated with elevated overtraining risk — Pfitz's mid-to-high plans regularly exceed this.

**Implication for Athlos:** Recovery weeks at ~70% of prior peak volume (not just the prior week). The quality session count should drop to 0 in recovery weeks. Hard days must be genuinely hard; easy days must be genuinely easy.

## The Corrected Periodization Model for Athlos

| Phase | Focus | Quality Sessions | Strength |
|-------|-------|-----------------|----------|
| General Fitness | Aerobic base | None | 2x/week |
| Base | Aerobic volume + introduce VO2max | 1x tempo OR 1x intervals | 2x/week |
| Build | Threshold + VO2max + MP introduction | 1–2x (tempo + intervals; MP enters late Build) | 2x/week |
| Peak | Marathon specificity | 2x (MP dominant; reduce VO2max) | 1–2x/week |
| Taper | Freshen, maintain feel | 1x light tempo | 1x/week |

### Key differences from classic Pfitz:
1. VO2max intervals appear in Base/Build (raise the ceiling early), not Peak
2. MP work increases through Build and dominates Peak
3. Strength is scheduled, not supplementary
4. Easy runs are genuinely easy (no gray-zone medium-long at endurance pace by default)
