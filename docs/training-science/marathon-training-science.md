# Marathon Training Science

This document defines the training science principles that govern Athlos plan generation. It is grounded in peer-reviewed research (2022–2024 meta-analyses where available) and modern elite coaching methodology. Where Pfitzinger's *Advanced Marathoning* conflicts with this document, this document wins.

---

## What Determines Marathon Performance

Marathon performance is primarily determined by three factors in descending order of race-day relevance:

1. **Lactate threshold (LT)** — the pace you can sustain without accumulating lactate. Correlates with marathon time at r=0.91 in recreational runners.
2. **Running economy (RE)** — how efficiently you convert oxygen to forward movement. At the sub-elite level, RE may matter as much as LT.
3. **VO2max** — sets the ceiling. A higher ceiling means more room for LT and RE development, but VO2max alone doesn't win marathons.

Training emphasis should follow this order. VO2max work raises the ceiling early; the final weeks belong to LT and marathon-specific work.

---

## Intensity Distribution

Elite marathon runners predominantly train with a **pyramidal distribution** — Zone 1 (easy) > Zone 2 (moderate) > Zone 3 (hard) — particularly during base phases. As the race approaches (final 8 weeks), training shifts toward **polarized** — minimizing Zone 2 gray-zone work in favor of a sharper easy/hard contrast.

The strict 80/20 polarized framing is an oversimplification. Observational data on 119,452 marathon runners shows pyramidal is the dominant pattern among fast runners. However, the underlying principle — **avoid the gray zone** — is well-supported.

**By phase:**
- **Base:** Zone 1 dominant. Zone 2 moderate work acceptable. Avoid making easy days moderately hard.
- **Build:** Zone 1 dominant. Quality sessions clearly Zone 3. Reduce Zone 2.
- **Peak:** Polarized. Easy days genuinely easy. Hard days at threshold or MP. Gray zone minimized.
- **Taper:** Zone 1 dominant, 1 light quality session to maintain feel.

**Sources:** Filipas et al. (SJMSS 2022): [PMC9299127](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9299127/) · Polarized vs. threshold meta-analysis (2024): [PMC11329428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11329428/) · Marathon runner intensity distribution (PubMed 2024): [39616560](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39616560/)

## Periodization

### Phase Structure

| Phase | Duration | Quality Focus | Sessions/Week |
|-------|----------|---------------|---------------|
| General Fitness | Optional, 20+ week plans | None — aerobic base only | 0 |
| Base | ~30–40% of plan | Threshold/tempo (introduce LT work) | 1 |
| Build | ~25–30% of plan | Threshold + VO2max intervals (sharpener); MP enters late Build | 1–2 |
| Peak | ~15–20% of plan | MP dominant + threshold support; VO2max reduced/eliminated | 2 |
| Taper | 3 weeks | 1 light tempo in week 1; 0 after | 0–1 |

### Why This Order

**Threshold before VO2max, not the reverse.** Threshold work builds the aerobic base and sustainable pace foundation first. VO2max intervals are most effective as a sharpening stimulus in mid-Build after threshold capacity is established. The final 6–8 weeks should be marathon-specific: MP work dominates, VO2max reduced or eliminated.

Running VO2max intervals in Peak is a Pfitz-specific choice that is not optimal for marathon preparation. VO2max intervals are most relevant to 5K–10K racing.

**MP work must begin early enough.** A 2024 analysis of 92 sub-elite marathon training plans (PMC11065819) found runners average 50–75 total miles at marathon pace over the final 12 weeks. Pfitz's ~14 total miles of MP work across 12 weeks is dramatically below evidence-based plans. MP should enter in late Build and dominate Peak.

### Quality Sessions by Phase

| Phase | Session 1 | Session 2 | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-----------|-------|
| General Fitness | — | — | Easy volume only |
| Base | Tempo (threshold) | — | 1×/week |
| Build (early) | Tempo | Intervals (VO2max) | 2×/week as fitness builds |
| Build (late) | Tempo | MP | MP replaces intervals |
| Peak | MP | Tempo | MP is primary; tempo supports LT |
| Taper W1 | Light tempo | — | Short, easy; maintain feel |
| Taper W2+ | — | — | Easy only |

### Recovery Weeks

Recovery weeks every 4th week: 30–40% volume reduction from prior peak. For advanced runners, 1 short easy quality session is acceptable and common in elite programs in Base/Build recovery weeks. 0 quality sessions in taper recovery.

**Sources:** Sub-elite marathon plan analysis (PMC11065819, 2024): [link](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11065819/) · Factors influencing marathon performance (Frontiers Cardiovascular Medicine, 2022): [link](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cardiovascular-medicine/articles/10.3389/fcvm.2022.856875/pdf)

## Long Runs

Two categories:

1. **~75% of long runs: genuinely easy.** Zone 1, 55–75% of 5K pace. Primary purpose: mitochondrial development, fat oxidation, tendon/connective tissue adaptation.
2. **~25% of long runs (1 every 3–4 weeks in Build/Peak): include MP segments.** Format: fast-finish (last 25–30% at MP) or interspersed MP intervals. Builds specific marathon fatigue tolerance.

Peak long run: 20–22 miles (32–35 km). Total training load, not peak long run distance, best predicts late-race performance. Cumulative volume in the 12-week build matters more than whether the peak run is 20 or 22 miles.

## Quality Session and Strength Day Placement

### Tempo Placement: Wednesday

Athlos uses Wednesday for the single quality session in Base (and the primary quality session in Build/Peak). Pfitz uses Tuesday — also physiologically sound — but Wednesday produces a better overall week structure:

1. **Better strength placement.** Wednesday tempo creates two non-adjacent strength slots: Monday and Friday. Tuesday tempo leaves only Thursday + Friday as eligible strength days, and those are adjacent, so only one can be selected.
2. **Three days post-long-run.** One additional day of recovery after the long run vs. Tuesday. Marginal for advanced runners, but the direction is correct.
3. **Used by elite programs.** RunnersConnect elite training schedules and several sub-3 hour marathon programs use Wednesday as the key midweek quality day.

### Resulting Week Structure

| Day | Base | Build early | Build late | Peak |
|-----|------|-------------|------------|------|
| Mon | Easy + Strength | Easy + Strength | Easy + Strength | Easy + Strength |
| Tue | Easy | Easy | Easy | Easy |
| Wed | Tempo | Tempo | Tempo | MP |
| Thu | Easy | Easy | Easy | Easy |
| Fri | Easy + Strength | **Intervals** | **MP** | Tempo |
| Sat | Rest | Rest | Rest | Rest |
| Sun | Long | Long | Progression | Progression |

In Build weeks with two quality sessions, Friday carries the second quality session and becomes ineligible for strength. Monday is the sole strength day those weeks. This is correct — high-intensity weeks deprioritize the second strength day.

## Strength Training

### Why It Matters

Strength training is a **first-class performance intervention**, not supplementary. The 2024 meta-analysis (PMC11052887, 31 studies, 652 runners) places its effect on running economy in the same tier as lactate threshold work.

Three mechanisms explain the RE gains:

1. **Neuromuscular efficiency.** Heavy resistance improves rate of force development — less relative muscular effort per stride at marathon pace, delaying late-race fatigue. This is a neural adaptation that does not require meaningful hypertrophy; high-intensity, low-volume protocols (≥85% 1RM, 3–5 sets of 3–6 reps) outperform bodybuilding-style training for runners.

2. **Tendon stiffness and elastic energy return.** Resistance training increases stiffness in the Achilles and patellar tendons. Stiffer tendons store and release more elastic energy per ground contact, improving RE without additional metabolic cost.

3. **Running economy — the downstream effect.** PMC11052887 (2024): combined heavy + plyometric training produced ES = −0.426 for RE (moderate effect); heavy load alone: ES = −0.266. Plyometrics alone: not significant at marathon-relevant speeds. PMC9653533 (2022): heavy resistance outperforms plyometrics for RE (g = −0.32 vs −0.13) and time-trial performance (g = −0.24 vs −0.17). Optimal protocol: ≥80–90% 1RM, 10–14 week interventions.

### Scheduling Rules

- **Easy run days only.** Never on quality session days (tempo, intervals, MP).
- **After the run, not before.**
- **Not adjacent to quality sessions (either side).** Pre-quality compromises performance; post-quality doubles recovery demand.
- **Not the day before the long run.** The day *after* the long run is acceptable — the concern is pre-effort fatigue, not post-effort recovery.
- **Minimum 48 hours between resistance sessions.**

### Frequency by Phase

| Phase | Resistance | Core |
|-------|------------|------|
| General Fitness, Base, Build | 2×/week* | 3×/week |
| Peak | 1×/week | 2×/week |
| Taper Week 1 | 1×/week | 1×/week |
| Taper Week 2+ | 0 | 0 |
| Race Week | 0 | 0 |

*Subject to quality session displacement — may be 1×/week when two quality sessions are present.

### Phase-by-Phase Loading

| Phase | Goal | Intensity | Volume | Notes |
|-------|------|-----------|--------|-------|
| General Fitness | Build tissue tolerance | 60–75% 1RM | 3×10–12 | Sub-threshold by design; RE gain is not the goal here |
| Base | Hypertrophy → max strength transition | 70–85% 1RM | 3–4×6–10 | Heavy compound lower body, hip drive |
| Build (early) | Maximum strength | ≥85% 1RM | 3–5×3–6 | Peak load, low volume — where RE adaptations primarily occur |
| Build (late) | Strength → power transition | ≥80% 1RM + plyos | 3×4–6 + plyos | Explosive variants, single-leg; plyometric complement introduced here only |
| Peak | Maintenance | ≥80% 1RM | 2–3×3–5 | Minimal fatigue; preserve neural adaptations |
| Taper W1 | Keep motor patterns fresh | ≥75% 1RM | 2×4–6 | Reduced volume, maintained intensity — neural pattern retention |

**Why early phases are sub-threshold:** General Fitness and Base use 60–85% 1RM — below the ≥80% threshold where significant RE gains occur. Connective tissue adapts more slowly than muscle. Building tissue tolerance before heavy training reduces injury risk. The Base-to-Build transition is continuous, not a hard step.

**Why Taper W1 maintains load:** Volume reduces but intensity stays ≥75% 1RM. Volume reduction achieves freshness; maintaining intensity prevents neural detraining. Dropping load below 70% shifts toward hypertrophic stimulus and increases DOMS risk into Race Week.

### Example Exercises

- **Lower body compound:** squat, trap bar deadlift, Romanian deadlift
- **Unilateral lower body:** Bulgarian split squat, single-leg press, step-up
- **Hip and glute:** hip thrust, cable pull-through
- **Calf and Achilles:** heavy single-leg calf raise (seated and standing), loaded eccentric drop
- **Plyometric complement (Build late only):** box jump, broad jump, single-leg hop

### Plan Generation Anti-Patterns

1. No strength on quality days or adjacent to them — in either direction.
2. No strength the day before the long run.
3. No plyometrics as the primary intervention — plyometrics alone produce no significant RE effect at marathon-relevant speeds.
4. No high-volume strength in Peak — volume drops to maintenance.
5. No new exercises in Peak or Taper — novel movement patterns cause DOMS.
6. Don't start strength late — RE adaptations require 10–14 weeks.

**Sources:** [PMC11052887](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11052887/) · [PMC9653533](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9653533/)

## Volume Progression

The 10% weekly increase rule is a useful heuristic but **is not scientifically validated as a specific threshold.** A 2007 RCT found identical ~20% injury rates comparing the 10% rule vs. more aggressive ramp. What IS supported: avoiding single-session spikes above 110% of the prior month's longest run (increases injury risk 64%).

**Athlos approach:** Linear interpolation from starting volume to peak volume over the pre-taper weeks, with every-4th-week recovery dips at 70% of the target trajectory for that week (~30% reduction). This guarantees the plan reaches peak volume by the start of taper.

## What Pfitz Gets Right

- Mileage is the primary performance lever
- Long runs are critical; 20+ miles multiple times is sound for full marathon
- Phase structure naming and duration (Base → Build → Peak → Taper)
- Recovery weeks every 4th week
- Strength on easy days, never quality days

## What Pfitz Gets Wrong

1. **Periodization order:** Pfitz puts LT work early and VO2max late (in Peak). Correct order is threshold first, VO2max as mid-Build sharpener, MP dominant in Peak.
2. **MP volume:** ~14 miles of MP work in 12 weeks is dramatically below sub-elite norms (~50–75 miles). MP should dominate the final 6–8 weeks.
3. **Intensity distribution:** "General aerobic" runs at 75–84% max HR push into the gray zone — fatigue without commensurate adaptation.
4. **Strength as supplementary:** Pfitz presents strength as optional. It is a first-class performance intervention.

## Gaps and Uncertainties

- Phase sequencing order has limited RCT evidence specifically for marathon runners. Most evidence is observational or inferred from shorter-cycle endurance studies.
- Exact "correct" total MP mileage over 18–20 weeks is not established by controlled trials.
- Polarized training studies primarily use cyclists or shorter-distance runners; marathon-specific RCTs are limited.
- Whether 0 or 1 quality session on recovery weeks is optimal is practically unstudied in RCTs.
- Exercise selection: no head-to-head RCTs. "Heavy compound lower body" is well-supported; which specific lift is optimal is not established.
- Load periodization arc (hypertrophy → max strength → power) is theoretically grounded but no RCT has tested this sequence for marathon runners against alternatives.
- Plyometric minimum effective dose is unknown. Current guidance (Build late only, low volume) is conservative.
- Female runners: most meta-analyses skew male. Whether loading parameters differ meaningfully is an open question.
- Core training: frequency by phase is specified above; exercise selection and volume are not.
